# Source notes — what the infographics got right and wrong

The nine frameworks came from a set of social-media infographics ("by Natan Mohart", reposted on
TikTok). They are good summaries, but several attributions and statistics are wrong or unverifiable.
Use the citations below in any client-facing work. Verified October 2026. Several McKinsey "Enduring
Ideas" pages were read via McKinsey's mirror (karriere.mckinsey.de) because mckinsey.com returned
errors; they are cited under their mckinsey.com paths.

| Framework | Cite this | Infographic claim | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7S | Waterman, Peters & Phillips (1980), "Structure is not organization", *Business Horizons* 23(3):14–26, doi:10.1016/0007-6813(80)90027-0. Developed with Pascale and Athos (Peters, "A brief history of the 7-S model"). | "Tom Peters & Robert Waterman … 1980" | **Partly correct.** Omits co-author Julien R. Phillips |
| GE-McKinsey matrix | McKinsey Quarterly (MQ), "Enduring Ideas: The GE–McKinsey nine-box matrix", Sep 2008. Method critique: Wind, Mahajan & Swire (1983), *Journal of Marketing* 47(2):89–99, doi:10.1177/002224298304700209. | "Built by McKinsey for GE in the early 1970s" | **Correct** on date; "for GE" is from secondary sources only |
| Industry cost curve | MQ, "Enduring Ideas: The industry cost curve", Apr 2009. | "Built for commodities" | **Correct, with nuance.** McKinsey calls it "ideally suited to commodity products" and also usable where value differences are measurable |
| Business system | Bales, Chatterjee, Gogel & Puri, "Competitive cost analysis", **internal McKinsey staff paper**, Mar 1980 (per MQ "Enduring Ideas: The business system", Jun 2009). Porter, *Competitive Advantage* (1985) cites it. | "Created 1980 … 5 years before Porter's Value Chain" | **Correct, with caveat.** It was an internal paper, not a publication; Fred Gluck presented it publicly later |
| Three Horizons | Baghai, Coley & White (1999), *The Alchemy of Growth*, Perseus. Blank (2019), "McKinsey's Three Horizons Model Defined Innovation for Years. Here's Why It No Longer Applies", HBR, 1 Feb 2019. | "Not a sequence"; "fixed time frames aren't in the original" | **Correct.** Baghai (comment on Blank's blog) says each company sets its own timing and that the horizons "were never intended to be fixed" |
| Decision journey | Court, Elzinga, Mulder & Vetvik (2009), "The consumer decision journey", MQ, Jun 2009. | Statistics (below) | **Mostly correct**; one statistic overstated |
| Strategic control map | MQ, "Enduring Ideas: The strategic control map", Jul 2010 (Lowell Bryan). | "Developed 1996 by D'Silva, Fallon & Mehta" | **Matches McKinsey's own attribution**, but no public 1996 publication was found. Cite the 2010 MQ page |
| SCP | Mason (1930s); Bain (1956) *Barriers to New Competition*; Bain (1959) *Industrial Organization*; Scherer (1970) *Industrial Market Structure and Economic Performance*; Demsetz (1973), *Journal of Law & Economics* 16(1):1–9, doi:10.1086/466752; MQ "Enduring Ideas: The SCP framework", Jul 2008. | "McKinsey's SCP Framework"; "1980s McKinsey adds feedback loops" | **Wrong attribution.** SCP is industrial-organisation economics. McKinsey itself credits Mason, Bain and Porter, and claims only a 1980s "dynamic" extension. Two-way effects were already in the IO literature (Bain; Scherer 1970; Brozen 1971; Demsetz 1973; see Panhans 2024, *J. Hist. Econ. Thought* 46(3)) |
| Portfolio of initiatives | Bryan (2002), "Just-in-time strategy for a turbulent world", MQ, Jun 2002; MQ "Enduring Ideas: Portfolio of initiatives", Oct 2009; Courtney, Kirkland & Viguerie (1997), "Strategy under uncertainty", HBR Nov–Dec 1997. | "Lowell Bryan, early 2000s; builds on Three Horizons and Courtney's levels" | **Correct** |

## Decision-journey statistics (Court et al. 2009)
| Claim | What the article says | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ~20,000 consumers, 5 industries, 3 continents | "Almost 20,000"; autos, skin care, insurance, consumer electronics, mobile telecom | OK to cite |
| Initial-consideration brands up to 3× likelier to be bought | Same | OK, consumer context only |
| 2/3 of active-evaluation touch points consumer-driven | Same | OK, consumer context only |
| Car buyers add 2.2 brands to a starting set of 3.8 | Autos: 3.8 initial, 2.2 added | OK |
| "Up to 40% change their mind at point of purchase" | Up to 40% **of those who wait until they are in the store to decide** | **Overstated** in the infographic; quote the qualifier |
| 60%+ of facial skin-care buyers research online after purchase | "More than 60 percent" | OK |
| 6× gap in active vs passive loyalists among car insurers | "As much as a sixfold difference … among major brands" | OK |

**None of these describe B2B or public-sector buyers.** For B2B buyers, Gartner's buying-journey
research reports that buyers spend about **17% of buying time meeting potential suppliers**, all
suppliers combined (roughly 5–6% with any one supplier when comparing several). Buying groups
typically have **6–10 stakeholders**. Those figures come from a 2017 survey of about 750 buyers,
first published around 2018. Secondary sources were used because gartner.com blocked direct access.
Later Gartner material reports buying groups of 5–16 people. Quote with the year.

## The airline example (SCP infographic)
Airline Deregulation Act 1978 (Pub. L. 95-504) and the 2008–2013 merger wave (Delta–Northwest 2008,
United–Continental 2010, Southwest–AirTran 2011, American–US Airways 2013) are correct. "Four carriers
fly about 2/3 of US domestic traffic" is **out of date**: BTS data for Aug 2025–Jul 2026 shows the
big four at about **70% of domestic revenue passenger miles**. DOJ's 2013 complaint cited more than
80% of US commercial air travel, a different measure. In Malaysian client work use the local LSS
loop in `scp.md` instead.

## Bid/no-bid practice
- APMP Body of Knowledge (apmp.org) treats the bid/no-bid decision as a capture-stage gate based on
  return, win probability and risk. *Shipley Proposal Guide* (5th ed.) has a dedicated bid/no-bid
  section.
- Loopio & APMP, *RFP Response Trends & Benchmarks Report 2025* (1,544 respondents): average
  self-reported win rate **45%**; **83%** of teams use a go/no-go process; 87% of top performers
  (≥50% win rate) do. These figures are **self-reported and correlational**. No credible published
  statistic isolates the win-rate effect of qualified vs unqualified bids, so don't claim one.
