# Structure–Conduct–Performance (SCP) — why some markets pay and others don't

## What it is and where it really comes from
An industry's **structure** (concentration, barriers, buyer and supplier power, cost structure)
shapes how firms **behave** (pricing, capacity, R&D, M&A), and that behaviour drives **results**
(returns vs cost of capital, margins, exits). External shocks (technology, regulation, demand, input
costs) hit all three. SCP is **not a McKinsey framework**. It is the industrial-organisation
paradigm of Edward Mason (Harvard, 1930s) and Joe Bain (*Barriers to New Competition*, 1956;
*Industrial Organization*, 1959). Porter carried it into strategy with the Five Forces (1979–80),
and feedback effects from conduct and performance back to structure were already in the IO
literature (Bain; Scherer's *Industrial Market Structure and Economic Performance*, 1970; Brozen
1971; Demsetz 1973). McKinsey's own account credits Mason, Bain and Porter for SCP and claims only a
1980s "dynamic" extension (McKinsey Quarterly, "Enduring Ideas: The SCP framework", 2008). Call it
"SCP" in client work, not "McKinsey's SCP".

## When to use it
- Before advising a client to enter a scheme market (LSS round, CRESS, C&I rooftop, FPV).
- Before EcoEnergy enters a service market (BESS advisory, WtE, lender's TA).
- To play forward a policy shock: a new RFP design, an SAC reset, a tariff restructuring.

## What to measure, localised
| | Measure | Malaysian RE data sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Concentration (top-4 share of awarded MW; HHI where winner lists exist), barriers to entry (track record rules, Bumiputera packages, mandatory BESS, financing capacity, land and grid access), demand growth (quotas, NETR targets, data-centre load), buyer power (single buyer under LSS; corporate offtakers under CRESS), cost structure (capex-heavy, fixed-cost, near-zero marginal cost) | ST/PETRA RFP documents and award announcements; NETR; TNB annual reports; Bursa announcements by listed winners |
| **Conduct** | Pricing discipline vs aggressive bidding; lot sizing; consortium formation; M&A among developers; module sourcing; policy engagement through formal consultations | Award announcements, Bursa filings, industry press, ST public consultation papers |
| **Performance** | Project IRR vs the Energy Commission's preferred band; share of awards reaching financial close and COD on time; terminations; listed developers' ROIC vs cost of capital | Bursa annual reports; ST project status lists; `fin-model-engineer` |

## The full loop in one Malaysian market: Large Scale Solar, 2016–2026
(From EcoEnergy's LSS1–LSS6 article, Aug 2026; re-verify figures in `malaysia-context.md`.)
- **Shock:** competitive bidding replaces the Feed-in Tariff for utility scale (LSS1, 2016);
  global module prices collapse (by LSS3, 2019).
- **Structure:** many entrants, including landowners turned developers; modest quotas.
- **Conduct:** aggressive pricing. LSS3's lowest winning bid was 17.78 sen/kWh, roughly half of
  LSS2's clearing levels two years earlier.
- **Performance:** some winners struggled to close financing at their own tariffs; delivery
  bottlenecks in the 30-winner LSS4 round.
- **Feedback:** the regulator raises the bar each round. Bigger quotas and lots (LSS5 doubled to
  2,000 MW), local-ownership rules (LSS4 100% local; LSS5+ ≥51% local equity), a dedicated floating
  package (LSS5+), tariffs no longer disclosed, and in LSS6 solar+BESS hybrids (2,500 MW with
  1,250 MW / 6,000 MWh BESS), Bumiputera packages, domestic-module priority and a southern-region
  steer. Oversubscription fell from about 13× (LSS3) to about 2× (LSS5+). Structure shifts toward well-capitalised, multi-disciplinary bidders.
- **Implication:** in each round the advantage moves further from "cheapest panel" toward
  integrated capability (storage, financing, land, policy reading). Those are exactly the
  capabilities an independent multi-discipline advisor sells.

## Run it in four steps
1. Map today's structure **with numbers, not adjectives**.
2. List the three shocks most likely in the next five years (e.g. the SAC reset after the current
   regulatory period, an LSS7 design change, module/BESS price moves, data-centre demand).
3. For each, play forward: how will rivals behave, and what happens to margins and IRRs?
4. Pick the move that shapes structure in your favour, not just the one that wins this year
   (e.g. building BESS-integrated capability before it becomes table stakes).

## Where it's challenged
- **Chicago-school critique:** high profits may reflect efficiency, not market power (Demsetz,
  1973, *Journal of Law and Economics*). In LSS, a low-tariff winner may simply be better, not
  dominant.
- **Causality runs both ways.** Strong performers create concentration; it is not only that
  concentration creates performance.
- **Game theory has largely replaced it in economics.** In strategy work it is still a sharp first
  lens, but for bid-by-bid rival behaviour use the bid supply curve and scenario play.
- **Conduct must stay lawful.** "Pricing discipline" across competitors is not something to
  arrange. Coordinated pricing, market sharing and bid rigging are prohibited under the Competition
  Act 2010. Policy engagement goes through formal public consultations and industry associations,
  within the MACC Act 2009.
