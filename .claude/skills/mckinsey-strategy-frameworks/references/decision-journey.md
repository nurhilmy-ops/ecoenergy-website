# Decision journey — how clients actually choose (B2B and public-sector adaptation)

## What it is and where it comes from
The marketing funnel assumes buyers narrow their choices step by step. McKinsey's consumer
research (Court, Elzinga, Mulder & Vetvik, "The consumer decision journey", *McKinsey Quarterly*,
June 2009; about 20,000 consumers, five industries, three continents) found a loop instead:
**trigger → initial consideration → active evaluation (brands get added) → moment of purchase →
post-purchase experience → loyalty loop**. Brands in the initial-consideration set were up to three
times more likely to be bought, and about two-thirds of touch points during active evaluation were
consumer-driven (reviews, word of mouth) rather than marketer-driven.

**Adaptation warning.** That evidence is about consumers buying cars, insurance, skin care and
similar. EcoEnergy sells to buying groups (developers, landowners, financiers) and to public bodies
whose process is fixed by procurement rules. Use the *shape* of the journey; don't quote the
consumer statistics as if they described B2B or government buyers. For B2B, Gartner's buyer research
is the better reference. Buyers spend about 17% of buying time meeting potential suppliers, all
suppliers combined, and buying groups typically have 6–10 stakeholders (2017 survey; see
`source-notes.md`). Most of the journey happens where you are not in the room, so your published
evidence and your clients' and lenders' word of mouth carry the evaluation.

## Three journeys EcoEnergy needs to map
### 1. Private clients hiring an advisor (developers, IPPs, landowners, corporates)
| Stage | What happens | EcoEnergy's move | Track |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | An RFP announcement (LSS6), a rule change (CRESS SAC cut, SELCO revision), a tariff shock (RP4 bills), a lender asking for an independent study | Publish within days of the trigger (Insights articles) | Consult requests per article |
| Initial consideration | Client lists who comes to mind: their EPC, a Big 4 firm, an engineering consultancy | Be on lenders' and developers' shortlists before the need: lender panels, conference talks, published analysis | Share of invitations received vs RFPs known in the market |
| Active evaluation | Client asks peers and their banker, reads, compares | Make the independence argument checkable: anonymised cases, sample deliverables, lender references | Proposal-to-shortlist rate |
| Moment of purchase | Scope and fee; fear of paying for a dead site | Staged fee (screening first) lowers the commitment; free 30-min consult with KML pre-screen | Consult → proposal → work-order conversion (quotation register) |
| Post-purchase | Lender's TA tests the numbers | Defend the model through DD; never let a client face lender Q&A alone | Findings closed without rework |
| Loyalty loop | Next site, next round (LSS7), referral to a peer | Debrief, a follow-on screening offer, referral ask | Repeat-client share, referral share |

### 2. Public-sector and GLC buyers (consultancy tenders)
The journey is codified by procurement rules: needs and budget → tender or quotation → evaluation
committee → award → contract → delivery. **Influence ends where the tender begins.** Legitimate
"initial consideration" work happens before any tender:
- registration with the right bodies (MOF consultant registration, BEM practice registration,
  agency vendor lists);
- visible track record (published insights, conference papers, anonymised cases);
- pre-tender market-sounding sessions and industry briefings the agency runs openly.

During a live tender, communicate only through the channel the RFP names. Gifts, hospitality or
side-contacts with evaluators expose the firm to corporate liability under s.17A of the MACC Act
2009 (see `malaysia-context.md`).

### 3. The client's own customers (when advising a CRESS generator)
The corporate offtaker's journey: trigger (RE commitments, customer or export-market carbon demands,
data-centre tenant requirements) → shortlist of supply routes (Green Electricity Tariff, CRESS,
on-site SELCO/ATAP) → evaluation (delivered price vs grid tariff, SAC, firmness, contract term, credit
support) → contract → post-purchase (savings vs a moving grid tariff) → renewal or exit. Use it to
design the client's offer and to anticipate the exit points that matter to lenders (termination
clauses, volume risk); see EcoEnergy's CRESS bankability article.

## Audit in four steps
1. Ask recent clients (won and lost): where did they first consider you, and who did they add later?
2. Find the stage where you lose the most opportunities compared with your main rival.
3. Move effort there, even if it means less top-of-funnel spend.
4. Count active vs passive loyalists. Passive repeat clients (who rehire by default) are a rival's
   easiest win, so give them a reason to stay.

## Where it misleads
- Consumer statistics don't transfer to buying groups or regulated procurement.
- Small samples: a boutique has tens of clients, not thousands of respondents. Treat findings as
  qualitative and say so.
- Attribution is hard. A client may cite the free consult when a lender's referral did the work.
  Ask "who first mentioned us?" explicitly.
