Slope classes for solar: what Class III land means for your project — and why peat, limestone and ex-mining land count too.

Independent Advisory By EcoEnergy Consultancy Sdn Bhd Published 17 November 2026 ~9 min read

Most land offered for solar in Peninsular Malaysia is described the same way: "a bit hilly, but mostly usable." That sentence hides the question that decides your approvals, your study list and a large part of your civil cost: which slope class is the land in?

Peninsular Malaysia's hillside planning guidelines sort land into four slope classes. The class comes from the ground, not the seller's description, and the planning department checks it lot by lot when you apply. Under the guidelines it decides whether an EIA is needed, how much the specialist reports have to prove, and whether the state's environmentally sensitive area committee sees your file. This piece explains the classes, the conditions that push land up a class, and how to read your own site before you commit to it.

The four classes, by angle

The classes come from the 2005 highland development guideline of the then Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment. They were carried into the KPKT hillside planning guideline of 2009 and into PLANMalaysia's 2017 implementation guide for hill-slope applications. On angle alone, they read like this. The three documents word the extra conditions slightly differently; where it matters below, we say which one we are quoting.

ClassIn-situ slopeSame slope as a gradientGeotechnical constraint
Class Iunder 15°under 26.8%Low
Class II15° to under 25°26.8% to 46.6%Moderate
Class III25° to under 35°46.6% to 70.0%High
Class IV35° and over70.0% and overExtreme

Watch the units. Engineers and tracker datasheets often quote slope in percent; the guidelines use degrees. A "15% slope" is about 8.5°, comfortably Class I. A "15° slope" is 26.8%, and is the start of Class II. Mixing the two is a common error in land offer documents.

The class is not only the angle

The angle bands above apply where the ground shows no signs of erosion or slope instability. The guidelines then list conditions that put land into a higher class than its angle alone would give:

  • Class II also covers land under 15° that shows erosion or instability or is made of colluvium or other sensitive geology, and flood-threat areas.
  • Class III also covers 15° to 25° ground with moderate-to-severe erosion or signs of instability (the 2009 guideline as reproduced in PPA 13), and, more importantly for solar, areas of limestone, swamp, peat and former mining land, plus mudflood-threat areas. That last group is Class III in every version.
  • Class IV also covers 25° to 35° ground with erosion or instability, and debris-flow threat areas (2009 guideline).
  • Hilltops and ridges are Class IV in the 2005 table and Class II in the 2009 guideline. Treat a ridge as Class IV until the planning department says otherwise. The 2009 guideline also limits hilltop development to eco-tourism and recreation without earthworks.

Why this matters for solar: some of the flattest land offered for solar in the Peninsula is coastal peat, swampy lowland, or ex-tin-mining land with ponds that look ideal for floating PV. Under the guideline's own wording, those areas fall in Class III however level they are. A desktop slope map will show them as Class I. Only the geology tells you otherwise.

The guideline's stated purpose is hill, highland and hill-slope development, so a planning department may not apply its classes to a flat lowland lot at all. How it treats a floating array on a mining pond, as against the land around it, is a question to put to it early. Either way, expect peat, limestone and ex-mining ground to draw the geotechnical scrutiny the class implies.

What the class changes

1. Whether you need an EIA

The 2009 KPKT guideline is explicit. In Class I and Class II, an EIA report is needed only if the project is a prescribed activity under the Environmental Quality Act 1974 (the guideline cites section 34A; check DOE's current prescribed-activity list with your EIA consultant). In Class III, an EIA report is required for all development. In Class IV, a detailed EIA is required for all development. This is a planning requirement; whether DOE treats the project as a prescribed activity is a separate test. On schedule, it is the step that separates a Class II site from a Class III one.

2. Which specialist reports the authority will ask for

The same guideline lists five technical reports to submit with the application. The list is the same for all four classes; what changes with the class is how much the reports have to prove:

  1. A geotechnical investigation and slope-stability analysis by a geotechnical engineer.
  2. Geological and geomorphological mapping by a geologist registered with the Board of Geologists.
  3. A drainage report to the MSMA urban stormwater manual, by a hydrology engineer registered with JPS.
  4. An EIA by a DOE-registered consultant for development over 50 hectares (the general rule; the class rule above applies on top).
  5. An earthworks plan by an engineer registered with the Board of Engineers.

Each is a separate consultant and fee. Most take weeks; an EIA usually takes months once DOE review is counted. They are cheap next to a redesign, but they belong in your development budget from the start, not discovered at submission.

3. Who sees your file

PLANMalaysia's 2017 guide for applications under paragraph 22(2A)(c) of the Town and Country Planning Act 1976 (Act 172) says development is allowed only in Class I and Class II, and that development in Class III and Class IV (25° and over) is not encouraged. Hill-slope areas in Class III and IV, and land above 300 m, are marked as environmentally sensitive areas (KSAS) in local plans, and applications touching them go to the State KSAS Technical Committee, which decides whether the file must also go to the National Physical Planning Council.

The guide's referral criteria are written for housing and townships, such as more than half the development area at 25° or steeper. For agriculture and "other" development, which is where a solar farm most likely sits, the state committee sets the criteria. In practice that means a large Class III share puts your planning outcome in a committee's hands rather than a checklist's.

4. Elevation, separately

The same guide also bands land by height. Above 1,000 m no development is allowed. Between 300 m and 1,000 m it is not allowed except sustainable logging and low-impact eco-tourism. Between 150 m and 300 m development is allowed, but the local authority approves it only after the state KSAS committee's recommendation and, where relevant, advice from the National Physical Planning Council. The 2009 guideline exempts low-intensity infrastructure of national interest, including electricity, from the bans above 300 m; do not assume a solar farm counts. Few solar sites are that high, but upland estates in Pahang and Perak can be.

What it means on the ground

Planning is half the story. A rising share of Class II and III land also changes the engineering. With modules tilted south, rows on north-facing ground need wider spacing to avoid shading each other, and east- or west-facing ground adds morning or afternoon row shading and cross-slope the racking has to absorb. Steeper ground means more earthworks, longer posts on the downhill side, more erosion and sediment control, and longer access roads. The 2009 guideline adds a planning limit: on hill land, cutting is kept to roads and infrastructure, and cutting a hill to level the whole site is not allowed, so the layout has to follow the ground. Single-axis trackers generally tolerate less north–south slope than fixed-tilt racking, so check the manufacturer's limits before you assume a tracker yield. Up to Class II, none of this makes a site unbuildable; it makes it cost more per megawatt. The share of each class tells you where that cost will sit, not how much it is. That takes a survey and a layout.

How to read your own site

Before you sign a land option, a lease or a letter of intent, put three things on one page:

  1. The slope mix by class. What share of the land is Class I, II, III and IV by angle. Our free terrain screening tool does this from a KML or KMZ in minutes, with an elevation map, cross-sections and a PDF report.
  2. The geology. Check the JMG geological map for limestone and alluvium over old mining ground, the Department of Agriculture soil map for peat, and the title and old mining-lease records for ex-mining land. That is what moves flat land into Class III, and no slope map can see it.
  3. The flood and erosion picture. A site walk for gullies, slips and standing water, and a look at the JPS flood maps for the area. Also check JMG's slope hazard and risk map (PBRC), if one exists for your local authority area; PPA 13 uses it to mark sensitive areas. Either can lift land into Class II or III.

Two cautions on any desktop slope map, ours included. It uses open satellite elevation data at about 30 m resolution, which includes tree canopy and buildings, so the edges of plantation blocks and tree lines can read steeper than the ground beneath them. The 30 m grid also smooths short, steep features such as gully banks, river terraces and old cut faces, so they can read gentler than they are. And it classifies by angle only. It is a screen to decide what to study next, not a topographic survey or a geotechnical investigation.

The short version

If most of the site is…Expect…
Class I, on stable mineral soilA standard planning path; EIA only if the project is a prescribed activity
Class IIBuildable, with more earthworks, drainage and geotechnical scope
Class III by angleA full EIA under the 2009 guideline, the state KSAS committee, and development PPA 13 does not encourage
Flat, but peat, swamp, limestone or ex-miningClass III on the guideline's wording; ask the planning department whether it applies to your lot
Class IVA detailed EIA. The 2009 guideline does not permit development except low-intensity infrastructure, and PPA 13 does not encourage it; exclude it from the layout. If Class IV is under 10% of a mixed site, the 2009 guideline lets you apply for an exemption with stricter engineering
A hilltop or ridgeClass IV in the 2005 table, Class II in the 2009 guideline; the 2009 guideline limits hilltops to eco-tourism and recreation without earthworks

The slope class is one of the cheapest checks you can run on a site, and one of the few that can change the answer outright. Know it before you commit.
