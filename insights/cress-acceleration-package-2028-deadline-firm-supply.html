The CRESS Acceleration Package: can your project make 31 December 2028 — and does going firm pay?

Independent Advisory By EcoEnergy Consultancy Sdn Bhd Published 25 November 2026 ~9 min read

Since 18 November 2026, a CRESS project that supplies firm power can lock in a System Access Charge of 14 sen/kWh for 10 years from its commercial operation date (COD) — against a standard 20 sen for firm and 40 sen for non-firm supply in the current regulatory period. The catch is the date: the project must reach COD by 31 December 2028, and there are no extensions. From today that is about 25 months.

Our CRESS bankability article covers what a lender will ask once the deal is on the table. This one asks two earlier questions that decide whether the package is worth chasing at all: can your project physically get there in time, and does the battery that "firm" requires pay for itself?

The package in brief. It is set out in the Energy Commission's revised CRESS Guidelines (GP/ST/No.40/2024 (Pin.1/2026), dated 17 November 2026, Appendix 3) and in force from 18 November 2026.

It applies to firm supply only. For a solar plant, firm means directly connected storage of at least 50% of registered capacity for four consecutive hours, dispatchable by the Grid System Operator. COD must come by 31 December 2028; a project that misses it loses the package and pays the charge then in force. The contract with the green consumer must run at least 10 years. The green consumer pays its own separate charge, which the Energy Commission put at 4 sen/kWh for the current regulatory period (RP4) and which is reviewed each period. Existing registered projects can opt in by request to the Single Buyer, but a project registered as non-firm cannot later switch to firm.

Question 1: can you reach COD by 31 December 2028?

Twenty-five months is enough time to build a solar-plus-battery plant. It is rarely enough time to develop one from scratch. The record of earlier large-scale solar rounds is the best guide we have:

  • LSS3 (target COD 2021): plants reached COD between March 2022 and June 2024.
  • LSS4 (shortlisted March 2021): the COD deadline was extended to 31 December 2023, and a large share of the round was still not online in early 2024. The causes cited were a 132 kV connection backlog, licensing changes, rising module prices, and post-COVID interest and exchange rates.
  • TNB Kuala Muda Solar (500 MWac): its sukuk received a preliminary rating in March 2026 against a scheduled COD of 30 November 2027 — about 20 months from the financing stage to operation, for the national utility's own subsidiary.

Note, too, who is not aiming at 2028. Johor's own 4 GW solar target for Kota Tinggi and Mersing is phased from mid-2029, and the Johor solar-plus-storage projects Singapore approved for export in August 2026 expect to operate around 2029. The package rewards projects that are already in motion, not ideas.

Work back from the deadline

The milestones below are our planning view, not regulatory deadlines. They assume a plant of 30–100 MWac with storage, and they leave a few months of buffer, because "no extensions" means the buffer is the only protection you have.

A simple test: if you do not yet have land control, a grid study under way and an offtaker in term-sheet talks, treat the package as upside, not as your base case. Model the project at the standard firm charge, and treat 14 sen as a bonus if you make the date. If the project only works at 14 sen, it is a bet on the calendar.

Missing the date is not the end of the project, but it changes its economics for good. A project that misses COD pays the charge in force at the time — after RP4 ends in December 2027, that is whatever the next regulatory period sets. On the current guideline a revision can move the standard rates by no more than 15% (so up to 23 sen firm and 46 sen non-firm), but PETRA has said it will revise the SAC-setting principles from RP5. Run the "missed deadline" case before you commit, not after.

Question 2: does going firm pay?

Firm supply needs a battery: 50% of registered capacity for four hours, or 2 MWh of storage for every MWac of the plant. Whether the charge saving pays for it is arithmetic, so here it is per MWac.

  • The saving. Non-firm supply pays 40 sen/kWh in RP4. Firm supply under the package pays 14 sen, and the green consumer pays its separate 4 sen — 18 sen in total. The difference is 22 sen/kWh on every unit delivered. (Looked at from the developer's side alone it is 26 sen, but a buyer comparing offers will net off its own 4 sen.)
  • The energy. We assume 1,650 MWh delivered per MWac per year — a typical Peninsular Malaysia plant with its panels sized above its inverter rating. On that basis the saving is about RM363,000 per MWac a year.
  • The battery. RHB's benchmark, quoted by The Star in August 2026, is about RM4 million per MW of storage — roughly RM1,000 per kWh for four-hour storage, or RM2.0 million per MWac of plant. On cost alone, that pays back in about five and a half years.

Simple payback flatters the battery, because it ignores what the battery costs to run. The table below adds O&M at 2% of battery capex a year, round-trip losses at 88% efficiency on one cycle a day, augmentation of 15% of the battery cost in year 8 (and again in year 16 for the longer case), and an 8% discount rate. It compares the present value of the charge saving with the present value of all battery costs:

The 21-year case assumes that after the package's 10 years the gap between firm and non-firm charges stays at today's 20 sen. Every number here is an assumption you should replace with your own; a 10% change in delivered energy moves the saving by 10%.

What it tells you: at today's Malaysian benchmark, the charge saving alone roughly pays for the battery over the package's 10 years, and pays clearly over a 21-year contract — the length data-centre buyers have been signing. If you can buy storage below about RM1,000 per kWh, firm supply wins outright. Above RM1,250 it depends on the contract outliving the package.

What the arithmetic leaves out

  • In favour of firm: a data centre running around the clock values evening power more than midday power, and the government's planned 30% renewable minimum for data centres — still being drafted by the Energy Commission — points buyers toward supply they can count on. At 40 sen, non-firm CRESS may not work at all for many projects, in which case the real alternative to firm is no project.
  • Against firm: a firm plant pays the non-firm charge for any period its storage is out of service, so battery availability is a revenue line, not just an O&M metric. The green consumer's 4 sen is reviewed every regulatory period, and the Minister told the Dewan Rakyat on 27 October 2026 that the government is prepared to re-engage industry on the fee structure. And after year 10, the developer pays whatever charge applies then.

Who should chase it

Where you areOur view
Land, grid pathway and offtaker talks in place; finance closable by late 2027Chase it. Register as firm, lock the battery supply and build the schedule backwards from 31 December 2028.
Registered CRESS project that is already firmAsk the Single Buyer to opt in, then re-test the schedule against the deadline.
Registered CRESS project that is non-firmThe package is not open to you: non-firm projects cannot switch to firm. Optimise the project you have.
Early-stage site: no grid study, no offtakerModel it at the standard firm charge. Treat the package as upside you might reach, not a foundation.
A buyer (data centre or manufacturer)Ask who carries the 4 sen charge and its future reviews, and what happens to your supply if the developer misses the date.

The package is a genuine improvement, and for advanced firm projects it is a strong reason to move now. But it is a deadline-driven incentive with no extensions, sitting on top of a battery that costs about as much as it saves over ten years at today's prices. Treat the date and the battery as the two numbers that decide the project — and test both before you sign anything that assumes 14 sen.
