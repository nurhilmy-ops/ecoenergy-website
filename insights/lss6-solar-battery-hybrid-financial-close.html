LSS6 after the bid: what lenders will test in a solar-plus-battery project before financial close.

Independent Advisory By EcoEnergy Consultancy Sdn Bhd Published 25 November 2026 ~10 min read

LSS6 bidding is over. The Edge Malaysia Weekly (9–15 November 2026) reported that the tender concluded in late October and that winners may be named as early as January 2027; Maybank Investment Bank had earlier pencilled in shortlisting for the first half of 2027 and awards in the second. The Energy Commission has not yet published the closing date, the number of bids or a shortlist. Whatever the date, the clock that matters starts when the letter arrives: PETRA wants every LSS6 plant running no later than 31 December 2029.

This round is different in one way that changes the bank conversation. In Pakej 1 and 2, every winner must finance a battery next to its solar plant — 1,250 MW / 5,000 MWh of storage across the round, four hours at full output, alongside 2,500 MW of hybrid solar. PETRA did not even require bidders to have battery experience, an acknowledgement that few Malaysian sponsors, contractors or lenders have done this at scale. This article sets out what a credit committee will test, what the earlier rounds teach about delays, and what to have ready before the letter lands.

A note on the storage figure. The Energy Commission's RFP notice of 24 July 2026 gives LSS6 as 2,650 MW of solar and 1,250 MW / 5,000 MWh of battery storage — four hours at full output. Some trade coverage has reported 6,000 MWh; that is not what the notice says. Size your battery, and your model, to the RFP and your own bid documents.

First, the history lenders will read

Before a bank looks at your model, it looks at how earlier rounds turned out. The Energy Commission's own figures, as at July 2025, compare what was shortlisted in Peninsular Malaysia with what is actually running:

LSS1 (2016)16 projects, 434.0 MW14 projects, 354 MW~82%
LSS2 (2017)30 projects, 506.4 MW26 projects, 466 MW~92%
LSS3 (2019)5 projects, 490.88 MW5 projects, 491 MW~100%
LSS4 (2020–21)30 projects, 823.06 MW27 projects, 726 MW~88%

The shares are our arithmetic; the Energy Commission gives no reasons for the gaps. Two lessons stand out.

Most projects get built — but rarely on the first date

LSS3's target was commercial operation in 2021. Solarpack's 90.88 MW plant reached COD in March 2022 and ENGIE's 100 MW Kerian plant in August 2022; Cypark's Merchang plant came in June 2024, more than two years after its original March 2022 date, and the company deferred a perpetual-sukuk payment to fund completion. LSS4 fared worse. In 2022 the government extended its PPAs from 21 to 25 years and its COD deadline to 31 December 2023 as relief; even so, The Edge reported in February 2024 that a large share of the round's 823 MW was still not online, and that PETRA had refused further extensions. The causes cited were a 132 kV connection backlog, licensing changes, module prices that had risen from about US$0.20/W at bidding, and post-COVID interest rates and exchange rates.

The cheapest bid is not always the winning bid

LSS4's lowest offer, 13.99 sen/kWh, was not shortlisted; the winners came in at 18.50–24.81 sen/kWh in Pakej 1 and 17.68–19.70 sen/kWh in Pakej 2. The evaluation looks past the price — and so will your lender. A tariff that wins the bid but cannot survive the bank's downside case is not a win; it is an expensive way to discover that a project is unbankable.

What changes when the plant has a battery

A solar plant is a well-understood credit: irradiance, modules, inverters, a PPA. Adding four hours of storage adds a second asset with its own wear, its own failure modes and its own share of the budget. On RHB's capex benchmarks quoted by The Star (RM3–3.5 million per MW of solar and about RM4 million per MW of storage), a 100 MW plant with the Pakej 1 ratio of 50 MW / 200 MWh of storage costs roughly RM500–550 million, and the battery is 36–40% of it. Lenders will test it accordingly.

1. Degradation and augmentation

Batteries lose capacity with every cycle and every year. The bank will ask how many cycles your dispatch implies, what capacity the supplier guarantees in each year, and how you will keep meeting the PPA's storage obligation as cells age. The answer is usually an augmentation plan — adding modules on a pre-priced schedule — funded by an O&M reserve, and the alternative is oversizing on day one. Either way, it belongs in the base case, not in a footnote.

2. Warranties that a bank can rely on

Expect lenders to want the battery supplier's guarantees — usable energy, power, availability and round-trip efficiency, with the point where it is measured — backed by a creditworthy counterparty and assigned to the project company. The World Bank's ESMAP guidance on battery warranties lists the terms that matter: warranted power and energy, efficiency, calendar life and throughput limits, duty cycle and the long-term service agreement. A warranty that lapses if you exceed a cycle limit your PPA requires is worth less than it reads.

3. How the battery earns its money

The public LSS6 documents do not say how storage is dispatched or paid for — whether everything sits in one energy tariff or the battery earns something separately. Your PPA will. Model each dispatch obligation, each availability test and each penalty explicitly, because that is where a lender's sensitivities will land. If the battery's revenue depends on how often the grid operator calls it, the bank will want to see a low-dispatch case too.

4. Fire safety and insurance

International lenders typically benchmark storage safety against NFPA 855 and require the system to be listed to UL 9540, with fire and explosion testing to UL 9540A — a test that, as EPRI points out, has no stated pass/fail threshold, so the results need an engineer's reading, not a tick-box. Confirm the requirements of the Malaysian fire authority and your insurer early: spacing, suppression and access can change the layout, and the layout changes the land you need.

5. Who answers when solar and storage disagree

A single EPC contract that wraps solar and storage gives the bank one counterparty; separate contracts leave an interface — who is liable when the plant misses its storage test because of the inverter, the battery or the controls? Lenders will also want the control software protected if the operator changes: a transferable licence or an escrow arrangement. Settle these in the contracts, not in the lender Q&A.

Terms that have moved since earlier rounds

Several LSS6 terms reported by industry players differ from earlier rounds. These come from The Star's report of 15 August 2026, not from published RFP text, so check them against your own documents:

  • Delay damages: RM1,000 per MW per day, capped at 180 days — against RM5,000 per MW per day in LSS5+. For a 100 MW plant that is at most RM18 million.
  • Shareholding moratorium: until the third year after COD, against seven to eight years from the letter of notification before. After that, changes still need TNB endorsement and Energy Commission approval.
  • PPA: a 21-year agreement with TNB, according to a developer's chief executive quoted in the same report.
  • Local content: a minimum of 20%, with priority for locally made PV modules (PETRA, 16 July 2026).

Read the lower delay damages carefully. At RM1,000 per MW per day, a six-month delay costs a 100 MW plant RM18 million in damages — real money, but rarely the biggest cost of being late. Interest during a longer construction period, a missed tax window and a tariff that no longer covers the capex usually cost more. Your lender will run the delay case on cash flow, not on damages alone.

The shorter moratorium matters for exit planning. If your plan is to sell down after COD, you can now plan for it sooner — and your lender will want the change-of-control and consent terms in its own documents to match.

How LSS plants are financed

Malaysian utility-scale solar has a well-trodden financing route: ASEAN Green SRI Sukuk under the Securities Commission's Sustainable and Responsible Investment Sukuk Framework, rated by RAM or MARC. Two recent examples show the shape:

  • Solarpack Suria Sungai Petani (LSS3, 90.88 MWac): up to RM305 million of ASEAN Green SRI Sukuk Wakalah maturing between 2023 and 2043, rated AA2/Stable by RAM, against a 21-year PPA.
  • TNB Kuala Muda Solar (500 MWac): up to RM1.05 billion of ASEAN Green SRI Sukuk Wakalah, given a preliminary AA+IS rating by MARC in March 2026, funding 80% of a RM1.3 billion project with a 21-year PPA and a scheduled COD of 30 November 2027.

Bank lenders, for their part, classify renewable-energy project finance as "climate supporting" under Bank Negara Malaysia's Climate Change and Principle-based Taxonomy. Neither route is new. What is new is putting a four-hour battery inside the credit, and on that, The Star reported in August that credit committees are scrutinising technology performance, warranty structures and degradation models. Expect the first LSS6 financings to take longer than the solar-only ones did.

Tax: check before you model

On the published rules, LSS6 projects have no clear Green Investment Tax Allowance route. The current GITA Project incentive for solar needs an award letter and a MIDA application before first capex and by 31 December 2026, and solar is absent from the 2027–2030 GITA Project list proposed in Budget 2027, which is still pending gazettal. Model without GITA unless MIDA confirms otherwise in writing. Our GITA article sets out the timing traps.

Before the letter arrives: a readiness checklist

WorkstreamWhat the lender will askWhat to have ready
Financial modelDoes the bid tariff survive the downside case?A lender-grade model reconciled to the bid, with low-yield, high-capex, delay and low-dispatch cases
Battery supplyWho guarantees capacity, availability and efficiency — and for how long?Term sheet with warranted energy, efficiency (and where it is measured), cycle limits, LTSA and a pre-priced augmentation schedule
EPC structureWho is liable when the plant fails its storage test?Wrap, or interface agreement between the solar EPC and the battery supplier; software licence or escrow
Safety and permitsDoes the design meet fire and insurance requirements?Fire-safety basis of design (NFPA 855, UL 9540 / 9540A results) and the insurer's view on layout
Grid connectionCan the connection be built by COD?Grid study status, substation works and lead times — the cause of LSS4's worst delays
Land and approvalsIs the site clean and in your control?Title, lease and conversion status; slope and drainage studies; approvals timetable
OwnershipCan the sponsors deliver the equity, and when can they exit?Equity commitment letters; consortium agreement aligned to the moratorium and local-content terms
Independent engineerWill the bank's engineer sign off on the battery?Early appointment, so commissioning tests, degradation and safety are reviewed before financial close

None of this argues against LSS6. The round is large, the policy direction is clear, and storage makes the plants more useful to the grid. But the winners will be judged twice: once by the Energy Commission on price, and once by their lenders on whether that price holds when the battery is old, the grid connection is late and the dispatch is lower than hoped. The second test is the one that decides whether the plant is built.
