Data centres and Johor: how much of the power demand is real — and what it means for your solar land.

Independent Advisory By EcoEnergy Consultancy Sdn Bhd Published 25 November 2026 ~10 min read

If you own land in Johor, someone has probably told you that data centres will make it valuable for solar. There is something to it. TNB has secured electricity supply for 61 data-centre projects totalling 8.35 GW, and Johor accounts for 29 of those projects and about two-thirds of the capacity. The state wants roughly 5 GW of new solar by 2030–31, the government has approved a minimum renewable-energy share for data centres, and Singapore has given conditional approval to import solar power generated in Johor.

But the headline numbers measure different things, the actual load is a fraction of them, and the constraint on new solar in Johor is mostly the grid, not the land. This article separates what is contracted from what is used, sets out why the load is pulling solar south, and ends with what actually makes a piece of land a solar site.

Read the headline number carefully. 8.35 GW is the supply TNB has secured for data centres — not the power they use. In June 2026 their actual load was 1,256 MW, about 5.7% of Peninsular Malaysia's record peak of 22,014 MW (6 August 2026). It has roughly doubled in a year, from 603 MW in June 2025, so the growth is real. The gap between contracted and used is real too.

Same boom, different numbers

Data-centre figures are quoted in at least six ways. Before you rely on one, ask which it is:

The figures are not contradictory; they measure different stages. But they are a long way apart. Actual load was about 22% of the capacity already connected in June 2026, on our arithmetic from TNB's own deck. The government has noticed. The connected-load charge — what a customer pays for capacity it reserves but does not use — was reported in November to have risen from RM8.50 to RM89.64 per kW, according to the trade ministry; TNB's website still showed the old rate at the time. Either way, the aim is plain: to stop "phantom" demand from tying up the grid. Projections deserve the same care. The national planning committee's projection for 2026 peak demand, 21.3 GW, is already below the 22.0 GW actually recorded in August.

The point is not that the demand is fake. Data centres consumed 4.0 TWh in the first half of 2026, against 1.9 TWh a year earlier, and already make up 6% of TNB's unit sales. The point is that a land offer priced on "8 GW of demand" is priced on the most optimistic of six numbers.

Why the load is pulling solar south

Johor is where the data centres are

Johor holds 29 of TNB's 61 data-centre projects and 67% of the secured capacity. The state government reports 65 approved projects, 21 of them operating and drawing nearly 1.1 GW, with another 4–5 GW needed for those under construction or planned. Wood Mackenzie estimates that data centres already use about a quarter of Johor's electricity, heading towards 40% by 2035.

Policy now points renewable supply at them

  • A 30% renewable minimum for data centres. The Deputy Prime Minister told the Dewan Rakyat in October 2026 that the government has approved a minimum 30% renewable-energy share for data centres, to be met through CRESS or on-site self-consumption (SELCO). The Energy Commission is still drafting the framework, no effective date has been set, and reports differ on whether the 30% is measured against capacity or consumption. Until the framework is published, treat it as direction, not a rule.
  • CRESS deals are already happening. TNB signed a CRESS supply contract with DayOne in June 2026 for about 1.5 GWp of solar with 2.2 GWh of storage, and a 750 MWp solar park is being developed under CRESS for the same customer. Bridge Data Centres was TNB's first CRESS partner, for a Johor campus, and an SD Guthrie–Gamuda–Gentari venture signed a term sheet in September 2026 for 680 MW of 21-year solar-plus-storage supply to a US technology company. The CRESS Acceleration Package, in force since 18 November 2026, favours exactly this kind of firm, battery-backed supply; see our analysis of its 2028 deadline and economics.
  • LSS6 is steered south. PETRA's LSS6 announcement prioritises high-growth areas, "khususnya di wilayah Selatan Semenanjung" — especially the southern Peninsula.
  • Johor has its own plan. The Johor Renewable Energy Policy 2030, launched in November 2025, came with an agreement between the IFC, PDT and Ditrolic to develop a Southern Johor Renewable Energy Corridor across about 2,000 km² of Kota Tinggi and Mersing — a first phase of up to 4 GWp of solar and 5.12 GWh of storage, aimed at hyperscale data centres and exports to Singapore. The state's stated target is about 5 GW of new solar by 2030–31: 4 GW in Kota Tinggi and Mersing, phased from mid-2029, and 1 GW in Segamat.
  • Singapore is buying. Singapore's Energy Market Authority gave conditional approval in August 2026 for 900 MW of imports from solar-plus-storage projects in Johor (Sembcorp 300 MW; Southern Solar Alliance, a Ditrolic subsidiary, 600 MW), with operations around 2029, and in October 2026 for a further 1.3 GW from a Sembcorp-led consortium drawing on Malaysian solar and Vietnamese offshore wind.

The bottleneck is the grid, not the land

Land in Johor is not scarce. Connection capacity is. TNB is spending about RM43 billion on the grid in the current regulatory period (2025–2027). Its 500 kV national grid backbone — about 500 km along the Paka–Kampung Awah–Bahau South–Kulai East corridor — started construction in August 2026 and is due by 2030, and it has tendered a new 500/275 kV substation at Pasir Gudang. Until that capacity arrives, Wood Mackenzie points to shortages of 132 kV intake substations and limited connection points for renewables in Johor.

We have found no published figure from TNB, the Energy Commission or the Single Buyer for how much new solar Johor's grid can take today, and where it can take it. Note also that TNB's Green Lane Pathway, which cut supply lead times for data centres from 36–48 months to about 12, is a fast lane for loads; we have found no equivalent published fast lane for generators. A solar project's connection is studied and built on its own timetable.

The practical consequence: two parcels a kilometre apart can have completely different values for solar, depending on which substation they can reach and how much room it has. Proximity to a data centre does not tell you that. A grid study does.

What could slow it down

  • Water. Johor stopped approving water-intensive Tier 1 and Tier 2 data centres in November 2025, set a water-use-efficiency limit below 1.8 in January 2026, and federal rules announced in November 2026 require at least two days of water storage. An ISEAS study (August 2026) found the water regulator approved under 18% of the 808 million litres a day requested by 101 data centres. Water, not power, may cap some campuses.
  • Chips. Malaysia has required permits to export US-origin AI chips since July 2025, the US was reported in November 2026 to be tightening licence rules for advanced chips going to Malaysia, and the trade ministry is weighing a law requiring data centres to declare the GPUs they import. Rules that slow the hardware slow the load.
  • Unused capacity. The connected-load charge increase is meant to make operators release capacity they are not using — which could shrink the contracted numbers even as real load grows.
  • Cancellations. Deals do fall through: UEM Sunrise's 350 MW data-centre deal with Logos at Gerbang Nusajaya was called off in June 2025. We have found no evidence of TNB supply agreements being cancelled at scale.
  • An undefined 30% rule. Until the Energy Commission publishes the framework, nobody knows whether it applies to capacity or consumption, from when, or to existing campuses.

What actually makes your land a solar site

A data centre down the road does not make land a solar site. A substation with room on it, a clean title and gentle ground do. Here is how a lender's adviser will look at your parcel:

FactorWhy it mattersHow to check it
Grid connectionDecides whether, when and how big a plant can connectDistance to the nearest 132 kV or 275 kV substation, then a connection study — the only real test of headroom
Slope and groundSteep, peat or ex-mining land changes approvals, earthworks and costA terrain screen first; see our slope-class guide
Title and land useLenders need tenure that outlasts the PPA, on land that can be used for a power plantTitle search, land category and conditions, lease term and any conversion needed
Size and shapeUtility plants need large, compact parcels — and firm CRESS or LSS6 plants also need room for batteriesLayout test against the plant size you are targeting, including storage and fire-safety spacing
Flood and drainageWater on site raises structures and cost, and can sink a projectFlood history, drainage paths and a hydrological study at feasibility stage
Scheme routeLSS, CRESS or a private sale decide tariff, buyer and lender appetiteA scheme-fit review: who buys the power, and on what terms

Get the first two answers before you sign an exclusivity or option with a developer, and before you price the land on data-centre demand. A cheap screen at the start is worth more than a premium negotiated on a number that turns out to measure something else.
